## The Clock You Cannot See

Every cell in your body knows what time it is.

This is not a metaphor. The molecular machinery of circadian oscillation — the interlocking transcription-translation feedback loops of CLOCK, BMAL1, PER, and CRY — runs in nearly every nucleated cell, from hepatocytes to hippocampal neurons. It is as fundamental to cellular identity as the genome itself.

And yet, when a physician prescribes a drug, the question of *when* it should be taken is almost always an afterthought.

## The Scale of the Problem

Approximately 40% of protein-coding genes show circadian expression patterns. This includes:

- **Drug targets** — the proteins that medications are designed to bind
- **Metabolic enzymes** — the proteins that break drugs down
- **Transport proteins** — the molecules that move drugs across membranes
- **Immune effectors** — the cells and signals that fight disease

The implication is staggering: a drug administered at 8 AM encounters a fundamentally different biological landscape than the same drug at 8 PM.

## Why This Is an Ethical Problem

If we know that timing affects drug efficacy — and we do, with decades of chronopharmacology research — then ignoring timing in treatment protocols is not merely suboptimal.

It is a failure of translation. A failure to move knowledge from the bench to the bedside.

A failure that costs lives.

## The Path Forward

Chronotherapeutics — the practice of timing treatments to biological rhythms — is not new. But it has been marginalised, treated as an interesting curiosity rather than a fundamental principle of pharmacology.

This must change. And it must change computationally, because the parameter space is too large for intuition.

The question is not whether biological time matters.

The question is why we've been ignoring it.
